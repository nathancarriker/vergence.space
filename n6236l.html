The Blue Feather

The wish that saved me, I destroyed.

read
Essay 02  ·  931 words

The whole room seemed to flash blue as it taxied by Pro Flight Air’s front window. It next appeared on the concrete pad beside the old hangar, spun around hard to the right, almost within its own wingspan, its tail swishing like a restless appaloosa’s. Then the propeller stopped, the canopy slid back, and the man who’d just made it all happen became all but nothing, an incidental prop in the show, as he practically levitated from the cockpit and jumped from the tacky black wing-walk on the left wing fillet.

The Yankee caught me staring through the window in the door, winked at me, and said, “Hey Nate…let’s go beat up some clouds!” The airplane was mischief, brandished.

Only a few weeks later, the incidental prop jumping from its wing was me, its new half-owner.

So tiny. So blue. Patriot Blue, American Aviation had called it, and by “blue overall,” they meant “blue all over,” with two simple, straight white stripes running from nose to tail, with the bottom one far thicker – like a contrail streaking across a horizon – broken only by the shorter-than-normal tail number N6236L dominating its shorter-than-normal aft fuselage, all carried around on wings 3/4ths the size of Cessna’s lightest free-flying kites.

The lack of wing area made it fly fast, which is to say, it had to go fast to fly. With only 27 horses in each of its Lycoming engine’s four cylinders, takeoffs were tentative. Climbs took commitment. But once the magic altitude was reached and the slipstream could get out of the coarse-pitched prop’s way, time’s edges would start to fray. Turbulence hit like a jab to a boxer’s body: tight, finite, then over, without consequence. Use of full control authority at anything even close to maneuvering speed was dizzying. Roll rate was 80 degrees per second. The word “glide” takes too long to say. Some would call it “the flying crowbar.” The wing flaps were purely decorative, but forward slips to landings were, to quote the Pilots Operating Handbook, “breathtaking.”

It would snarl about inattentive rudder use by dropping a wing in a stall – which wouldn’t be that big a deal if it weren’t for the fire-engine-red SPINS PROHIBITED placard glaring at me from front and center on the instrument panel – the result of a fatal crash during the design’s flight testing.

I could slide its canopy back just so far on a warm day before a spooky-sounding flutter would start, and any g-load above 1.0 would make it happen at exponentially slower speeds, making it kind of a poor-man’s g-meter.

One cylinder’s tighter compression would stop the propeller in the same place on shutdown, every single time.

Don a headset, start the engine, and turn on both the rotating beacon and #1 radio, and the headset became a stethoscope, and the ammeter became an electrocardiograph, carrying the pulse of the alternator’s perhaps two-ampere background whine, punctuated as rhythmically as any metronome by the beacon’s extra five amp draw for the bulb, about every two seconds.

“deedeedeedeeee, deedeedeedeeee…”

“So what,” you say. “Any under-designed ignition or electrical system would do that.” And you’re right. But in the Yankee, any time the old tube-type #1 radio would send any audio signal to the headset, an electrical event demanding not even a thousandth of the power capacity of that alternator, the beacon and its pulse would go dark and silent, as if the Yankee was turning the noise down to listen to whatever was going on out there. It would do the same any time I’d key the microphone to transmit, for as long as I held it down, so not even God – let alone anybody else on the frequency – could crash our little party.

To climb onto that tiny wing, flip up the seat bottom with my foot, exposing that one-piece, tubular steel spar running from wingtip to wingtip, clutching the fuel like a running back clutches a football, safe from all but total destruction, sit down, and slide that canopy forward wasn’t a process, it was a transfiguration. Man, become machine, become man.

I’d trained for and taken my commercial, instrument, and flight instructor checkrides in that one, unique airplane, which my secret, inner Richard Bach fan had dubbed The Blue Feather – because it appeared to me, a high school senior trying to figure out how in the world I was ever going to finance commercial pilot training, out of nowhere.

In Illusions, Don Shimoda loans Bach his “Messiah’s Handbook,” which always magically opens to a page that says what the reader most needs to hear. When he reads, “You’re never given a wish without also being given the power to make it come true. You may have to work for it, however,” Shimoda challenges Bach to imagine something random and watch it work. Bach closes his eyes and describes a blue feather, floating alone in space, but then opens his eyes, disappointed to see nothing new on the table between them – except on his milk carton, “packaged for Scott Dairies by Blue Feather Farms, Bryan, Ohio.”

We were tight, The Feather and me.

So as evening’s shadows swept up what little heat December 14th had given Martinez Canyon’s floor, we lay side by side for 18 final hours together, broken, useless, and flat on our backs, separated by only feet, but forever. That’s when the casualties I’d just made of us knew how Bach felt to watch the life drain from his friend’s murdered body and read on the page to which his handbook opened, “everything in this book might be wrong.”
